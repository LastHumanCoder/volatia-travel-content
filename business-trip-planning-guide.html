Business Trip Planning Guide: A Checklist That Survives Contact

Most business travel planning checklists are a packing list with a flight number on top. This one covers the things that actually cost you the meeting.

Good business travel planning works backwards from the meeting, not forwards from the flight. Decide what the trip has to achieve, then build the schedule so your people arrive able to achieve it: rested, with the right documents, and able to be understood.

The three things that most often go wrong are documents, jet lag against a critical meeting, and language. The first two are widely planned for. The third almost never is.

Six weeks out: decide what the trip is for

  • Write down the outcome. Signing, exploring, resolving, or maintaining a relationship. Everything downstream follows from this.
  • Identify who must be in the room, on both sides, and who actually decides.
  • Check passport validity. Six months beyond entry is a common requirement, and renewal is slow.
  • Start the visa process. Business visas take longer than tourist visas and often need an invitation letter from the host.
  • Confirm the language situation honestly. Not "they speak good English", but whether their English is good enough for contract terms under pressure. If you are not sure, assume it is not.

Four weeks out: book around the meeting

  • Arrive at least a full day early for anything important. A red-eye arrival on the morning of a negotiation is a decision to negotiate badly.
  • Book accommodation close to the meeting, not close to the airport. Traffic in most business capitals is worse than the map suggests.
  • Book interpreters now if any session needs a scheduled one. Simultaneous interpreters work in pairs and the pool for a given language and subject is small.
  • Check the local calendar. National holidays, religious observances and industry events will close offices or fill every hotel in the city.
  • Confirm what your traveller needs to work. Data roaming, a VPN where required, power adapters, and whether the tools you rely on are accessible in that country.

Two weeks out: prepare the substance

  • Send materials to your interpreter. Agenda, deck, draft contract, spec sheet and a glossary of product names and internal acronyms. This is the single highest-return twenty minutes in the whole process.
  • Translate anything that will be relied on. A contract or specification that exists properly in only one language is a dispute waiting for a date.
  • Brief your own team on meeting etiquette in that market: how directly to disagree, how business cards are exchanged, whether the first meeting is expected to conclude anything.
  • Set up on-demand interpreter access on every traveller's phone and test it before departure.
  • Confirm insurance covers business travel in that country, including medical evacuation.

The week before

  • Reconfirm every booking directly with the hotel and the ground transport, not just through the confirmation email.
  • Share the itinerary with someone at home who is not travelling, including flight numbers, hotel details and local contacts.
  • Take copies of documents, stored separately from the originals and accessible offline. See what to do if you lose your passport abroad.
  • Get local currency for the first day, and check whether cards are widely accepted.
  • Plan for the time zone. Shift sleep by an hour a day in the right direction for the few days before departure.

Building the schedule itself

Ignoring interpretation timeDouble the schedule for consecutive interpreting
No buffer before the flight homeHalf a day, because the last meeting always runs long
Dinner scheduled as an afterthoughtIn many markets dinner is where the deal moves. Plan it.

The language plan, in four lines

This is the part most corporate travel planning leaves out entirely, so here it is as its own checklist:

  1. Scheduled sessions: a booked interpreter, mode agreed, materials sent ahead.
  2. Unscheduled everything: on-demand phone and video interpreting on every traveller's phone, in 300+ languages, 24 hours a day.
  3. Documents: anything that will be relied on, translated properly rather than machine-translated.
  4. A fallback: someone at home who can reach both your travellers and an interpreter at any hour.

While you are there

  • Confirm the numbers out loud in every meeting. Repeat back figures, dates and quantities.
  • Write the summary the same day, while you still remember what was agreed and what was merely discussed.
  • Send the summary to the other side and ask them to confirm it. Misunderstandings surface here cheaply.
  • Keep receipts as you go. Reconstructing expenses two weeks later is nobody's idea of a good afternoon.

After the trip

Write down what did not work while it is still annoying: the hotel that was too far, the meeting that needed an interpreter and did not have one, the connection that was too tight. A company travel plan improves through that list and through almost nothing else.

Common questions

How far in advance should business travel be planned?

Start six weeks out for international trips. Visas, invitation letters and scheduled interpreters all have lead times, and arriving a full day before an important meeting means booking flights early enough to have that option.

What is usually missing from a business travel plan?

The language plan. Documents, flights and hotels are planned carefully, and then the assumption is made that everyone will understand each other. For scheduled meetings that means a booked interpreter, and for everything else it means on-demand access on every traveller's phone.

How much buffer should a business trip schedule have?

A full day between arrival and the first important meeting, around two hours between meetings in unfamiliar traffic, and half a day before the departing flight. If the meeting is interpreted consecutively, double the time you allow for it.

Should you translate contracts before travelling?

Yes, if the contract will be relied on. A document that exists properly in only one language while the understanding exists in another is the most common source of disputes months later.

Who should hold the itinerary besides the traveller?

Someone at home who is not on the trip, with flight numbers, hotel details, local contacts and copies of key documents. They are also the person who can reach an interpreter on the traveller's behalf if something goes wrong.
