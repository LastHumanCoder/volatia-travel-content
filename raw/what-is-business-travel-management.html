What Is Business Travel Management?

It is the discipline of making company travel cheaper, safer and less annoying, at the same time. The three pull against each other, which is why it is a discipline.

Business travel management is how an organization plans, books, pays for and looks after its employees' work travel. It covers policy, booking tools, supplier negotiation, expense handling, reporting and duty of care, and it exists to balance three competing goals: controlling cost, keeping travellers safe and productive, and not making the process miserable.

The gap in most programmes is language. Policy and booking are solved. What happens when a traveller cannot make themselves understood usually is not.

The three goals, and why they fight

  1. Cost. Cheapest flights, negotiated hotel rates, enforced advance purchase. Push this alone and travellers arrive exhausted on connecting flights and perform badly.
  2. Safety and duty of care. Knowing where people are, reaching them, getting them help. Push this alone and the programme becomes expensive and slow.
  3. Traveller experience. A process people will actually follow. Ignore it and travellers book outside the system, which destroys both of the other goals at once.

Leakage, where travellers book outside the programme, is the clearest sign the balance is wrong. It is almost always a symptom of the third goal being neglected.

Travel booking programs and tools

Most organizations use one of three models, and plenty use a mix:

  • Self-booking tool. Business travel booking tools with policy built in, so travellers book themselves within the rules. Efficient for simple trips, weak when anything is unusual.
  • Managed agency. A travel management company books on your behalf and handles changes. Better for complex and international travel, and the model most programmes end up with for senior travellers.
  • Hybrid. Self-booking for routine domestic travel, agency support for international, group and executive trips. This is what most mature programmes actually run.

The choice is less about software than about who absorbs complexity. A corporate travel booking system moves it to the traveller. An agency absorbs it. International travel has far more complexity to absorb.

Duty of care, taken seriously

Duty of care is the legal and ethical obligation to look after employees you send somewhere. In practice it means being able to answer three questions at any moment: where are our people, can we reach them, and can we help them.

Most travel management solutions answer the first two well. The third is where programmes quietly fail, because helping somebody requires understanding what is happening to them. If your traveller is in a hospital in a country whose language nobody at head office speaks, tracking their location does not help them.

Where to start if you have no programme

  1. Find out what you actually spend and where it goes. Most organizations are surprised.
  2. Write a short policy. One page that people read beats twenty that nobody does.
  3. Pick one booking channel and make it the easiest option, not merely the mandated one.
  4. Solve duty of care before optimising cost. It is the part with legal exposure.
  5. Add the language layer for international travel, because it is inexpensive and it is the gap.
  6. Measure, then negotiate. Supplier deals need volume data to be worth anything.

Common questions

What is business travel management?

The way an organization plans, books, pays for and looks after employee work travel. It covers travel policy, booking channels, supplier agreements, expenses, duty of care and reporting, balancing cost control against traveller safety and experience.

What is a corporate travel booking system?

A self-booking tool with travel policy built in, so employees book their own trips within the rules. It works well for routine domestic travel and less well for complex or international trips, where a managed agency usually absorbs the complexity better.

What does duty of care mean in travel?

The obligation to look after employees you send somewhere. In practice it means knowing where they are, being able to reach them, and being able to help them. The third is where most programmes fall short, because helping requires understanding the situation, which a language barrier prevents.

Do small companies need travel management?

Anyone sending people abroad needs the duty of care part, regardless of size. The policy and supplier negotiation parts only pay for themselves at volume. A one-page policy plus proper support for international trips covers most small organizations.

What is usually missing from travel management solutions?

Language support. Policy, booking and tracking are well covered by existing tools, but almost no programme gives travellers a way to reach a professional interpreter when something goes wrong abroad, which is inexpensive to add and often the thing that matters most.
