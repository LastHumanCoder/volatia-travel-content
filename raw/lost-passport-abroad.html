What to Do If You Lose Your Passport Abroad

It is recoverable, it usually takes days rather than weeks, and the order you do things in matters more than the speed.

If you lose your passport abroad: report it to the local police and get a written report, contact your country's nearest embassy or consulate, and apply for an emergency travel document. Most embassies can issue one within a few working days, and sometimes the same day if your flight is imminent.

Do the police report first. Most embassies will ask for it, and getting one afterwards means making the same journey twice.

The order to do things

A lost passport abroad feels like an emergency and is really a process. Work through it in this sequence.

  1. Stop and retrace. Most passports reported lost are found within a few hours, in a hotel safe, a bag pocket or with the front desk. Spend twenty minutes on this before starting the process.
  2. Report it to the local police and get a written report with a reference number. In many countries you also need this for an insurance claim.
  3. Contact your embassy or consulate. Find the nearest one, check their hours, and call before travelling there. Many now require an appointment.
  4. Gather what you can. A photocopy or photo of the lost passport, another form of ID, your travel itinerary, and passport photographs.
  5. Apply for an emergency travel document, which lets you travel home or, in some cases, continue a trip.
  6. Tell your airline and your insurer, and change bookings once you know your actual document date.

What an emergency travel document is

An emergency travel document is a short-term, single-purpose document that replaces your passport for the journey home. It is not a passport. It typically covers a specific route, is valid for a limited period, and some countries will not accept it for transit, which is why the route it lists matters.

Timing varies by country and consulate, from same day in urgent cases to several working days. A full replacement passport takes longer and usually has to be issued from your home country.

Rules, fees and processing times differ by nationality and by consulate and they change. Your own government's foreign office or state department is the only authoritative source. Check their website for the country you are in before you do anything else.

Then the part nobody plans for

Every step above assumes you can explain yourself. In practice, the police station may have nobody who speaks your language, the report will be written in a language you cannot read, and you will be asked to sign it.

That is a genuine problem. A police report is a legal document, and signing one you do not understand is not advisable at the best of times, let alone as the basis of an emergency travel document application.

  • Do not sign anything you cannot read. Ask for time and get it interpreted first.
  • Use a professional interpreter, not a bystander. A helpful stranger is not accountable and may not be accurate.
  • Get an interpreter on the phone rather than waiting for one to appear. On-demand phone interpreting connects in seconds in more than 300 languages, at any hour.
  • Ask for a copy of everything, and photograph it before you leave the building.

How to make this survivable before you go

  • Photograph your passport and store it somewhere you can reach without your phone, such as an email to yourself.
  • Carry a paper photocopy separately from the passport itself.
  • Take two spare passport photographs. Trivial at home, surprisingly awkward abroad.
  • Save your embassy's details for each country on your route, before you travel.
  • Leave a copy with someone at home who can send it to you.
  • Know how you would reach an interpreter, in advance, rather than working it out at a counter.
  • Never carry your passport when you do not need it. A hotel safe is safer than a pocket in a crowd.

If it happens to someone in your group

For faculty leads and trip managers, one participant losing a passport can pull the leader away from the whole group for a day or more. Decide in advance who stays with the group and who goes to the consulate, and make sure both have interpreter access. Common questions

What is the first thing to do if you lose your passport abroad?

Retrace your steps briefly, since most are found quickly. Then report it to the local police and get a written report with a reference number, because your embassy will usually ask for it and your insurer almost certainly will.

How long does an emergency travel document take?

Anything from the same day in urgent cases to several working days, depending on your nationality, the consulate and how busy they are. A full replacement passport takes longer and is usually issued from your home country.

Can I keep travelling on an emergency travel document?

Sometimes. These documents are often issued for a specific route home and some countries will not accept them for transit. Confirm with the issuing consulate before booking onward travel.

What if nobody at the police station speaks my language?

Get a professional interpreter on the phone before you sign anything. A police report is a legal document and signing one you cannot read is a bad idea. On-demand phone interpreting connects within seconds in more than 300 languages at any hour.

Does travel insurance cover a lost passport?

Many policies do, usually requiring a police report and often within a time limit for reporting. Check your own policy wording, because documentation requirements differ and some policies exclude items left unattended.
